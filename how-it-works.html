How it works

From a public camera to a warning on your phone

RipCo does not need new cameras. It reads a feed that already watches the water, runs a model over each frame and shows a likely rip as a red outline on the live view. This page goes through each stage, then through what the model can’t see.

The three stages

  1. It uses a camera that already exists

    Public beach cameras already stream the surf so people can check it from home. RipCo connects to one of those feeds. Nothing new has to be mounted or maintained, so adding a beach costs software and testing time.

  2. A computer-vision model reads each frame

    The model looks at the surface of the water for the signature of a rip: a darker lane cutting through the surf, a gap in the line of breaking waves, and foam or sand streaming out to sea. I trained it on footage from a public camera at Manly Beach.

  3. A likely rip is outlined on the live view

    When the model finds that signature it draws a red outline on the live image and shows its confidence as a percentage. A frame with no outline means it found no signature in that frame. The three things the Detect screen can show.

What the model looks for

A rip seen from above, and from the beach

Waves push water over the sandbars towards the beach. That water has to get back out, and it does so through the lowest point between the bars. That return flow is the rip.

A · Feeders
Water pushed over the sandbars flows along the beach from both sides towards the low point between them.
B · Neck
The flow turns and runs out through the gap. This is the fastest part: narrow, deeper and darker, with no waves breaking in it.
C · Head
Beyond the breaking waves the current slows and spreads out. Swimmers carried here can move sideways and swim back in.

The neck is the part a beach camera can see, and the part the model is trained on. The model looks for three cues, the same ones Surf Life Saving teaches people to look for.

Cue 1

A darker channel

Deeper water cutting out through the surf reflects less light, so a rip often reads as a darker lane running away from the beach.

Cue 2

A gap in the breaking waves

Waves break on the sandbars either side of a rip but not in the channel itself, leaving a calmer-looking gap in the line of white water.

Cue 3

Foam and sand moving out

Foam, sand and debris streaming away from shore show which way the water is pulling, which separates a rip from a patch of still water.

Limits

What it can’t see

These are the conditions where the model’s reading is weak or missing. In each of them the app marks detection as unavailable.

  1. Night, heavy rain and strong glare

    The camera image stops being usable, so the app marks detection as unavailable and keeps showing the conditions.

  2. Small, distant or oblique rips

    A rip that is small, far from the camera or at a shallow angle to it can be missed. A rip can be present without being detected.

  3. False positives

    The model can outline something that is not a rip, such as a shadow, a patch of weed or a lull between sets. The confidence figure is there so you can weigh each outline.

  4. Water that changes in minutes

    Rips form, move and fade with the sets and the tide, so an outline describes the frame it was drawn on.

  5. Beaches without a usable camera

    Detection needs a public camera with a clear view of the surf zone. Everywhere else the app shows conditions, and a live stream where there is a camera.

Whatever the screen shows

Swim between the red and yellow flags and follow the lifesavers and lifeguards on duty. RipCo can miss a rip; they are watching the water in person.

The safety commitment

The risk score

How the number on each beach card is built

Every beach card carries a score out of 100 with a label beside it. It summarises the conditions the app can measure, and it is deliberately framed as risk.

The score is built from the live conditions for that beach and shown with a label. On the morning of the screenshot on this site, Thursday 27 August, the Manly card read 55 and Moderate risk in amber, with water at 18.0 °C, air at 13.4 °C, wind at 19 km/h and a 1.2 m swell.

The label uses the safety colours for their meaning: amber for moderate. The band below Moderate is still a level of risk, and the word safe does not appear on the card. The patrol line sits under the score, so the reading and the instruction to check the flags arrive together.

Terms

The words this site uses

Short definitions of the terms used on this site and in the app.

Rip current
A narrow, fast channel of water flowing out through the surf, formed where water pushed over the sandbars returns to sea.
Feeders
The flows along the beach that gather water towards the low point between sandbars (A in Fig. 2).
Neck
The narrow, fast part of the rip running out through the gap in the breaking waves (B). The part a camera can see.
Head
Where the current slows and spreads beyond the breaking waves (C).
Sandbar
A ridge of sand under the surf where the waves break.
Sets and lulls
Waves arrive in groups (sets) with calmer gaps between them (lulls). A lull can look like a rip for a moment.
Swell
The height of the waves arriving at the beach, in metres.
Frame
One still image from the camera feed. The model reads the feed frame by frame.
Confidence
The model’s own estimate, as a percentage, that an outline is a rip.
False outline
An outline drawn on something that is not a rip. A missed rip is the opposite: a rip present with no outline.
Detection unavailable
The state the app shows when the camera image cannot be read, such as at night or in glare.
Risk score
The number out of 100 on each beach card, built from the live conditions and labelled as a level of risk.
Live
The tag on a beach card meaning the camera at that beach is streaming. It does not mean detection has been tested there.
Patrolled beach and the flags
A beach with lifesavers or lifeguards on duty. The red and yellow flags mark the area they are watching.
Common questions

Accuracy, night, coverage and location

How accurate is the detection?

Each outline carries a confidence percentage. The model misses some rips and outlines some things that are not rips, and the app never shows an all-clear, so assume a rip could be present in any session. The Manly pilot section is where the test record lives.

Does it work at night or in poor visibility?

Detection needs a usable camera image. At night, in heavy rain or in strong glare the app marks detection as unavailable and keeps showing the conditions. The rule on the sand does not change: flags first.

Which beaches have detection?

Manly Beach, where I trained and tested the model. Detection can extend to any beach with a public camera that gives a clear view of the surf zone. The coverage page lists the beaches this site tracks and where detection has been tested.

Does it use my location?

Location is optional. If you allow it, the app shows the beaches near you; without it, you can pick beaches from the map. More on the privacy page.
