Safety

Flags first, then the app

RipCo helps you read the water before you swim. These are the rules the app follows, and the questions a safety app has to answer about the camera it uses, the assistant it carries and the reports it shares.

The commitment

What RipCo will and will not do

Interviews with Surf Life Saving NSW patrol members shaped these rules. The commitment is mine. Nothing here is endorsed by Surf Life Saving.

  1. RipCo will never tell you a beach is “safe”

    There is no safe verdict anywhere in the app. It shows what it detects and what the conditions are. It never tells you the water is fine to enter, because an app cannot make that call for you.

  2. It adds to your judgement

    RipCo adds to what you can see with your own eyes and what the lifesavers and lifeguards tell you. Use it with both.

  3. Rip currents can appear in calmer-looking water

    A rip can form where the water looks smoothest, and the calmest gap between the waves is often where one is running. A calm surface tells you nothing about what the water is doing underneath.

  4. Detection can miss a rip, and can flag one that isn’t there

    A confidence percentage is a reading from a camera. Assume a rip could be present in any session.

  5. Always swim between the red and yellow flags

    The flags mark the patrolled zone, chosen by lifesavers and lifeguards reading the water in person. If there are no flags and no patrol, do not swim.

  6. Follow the lifesavers and lifeguards

    If their instruction differs from anything RipCo shows, follow them. They are watching the water in person and they have the final word.

In the app

The rule shows up as a line under each beach card: the patrol status where it is known, and “check the flags” in every case. See it on the annotated home screen.

The harder questions

Someone else’s camera, an assistant, and unverified reports

Never saying “safe” is the easy part. These are the questions a marker, a lifesaver or a journalist would ask next, answered as far as the prototype can answer them.

The camera

Whose feed it is

Detection runs on a public camera at Manly Beach that already streams to the web. The site and the app do not claim any relationship with its operator, and the footer says so. Reading a third party’s stream is the one arrangement that has to be settled before launch.

Ask RipCo

What the assistant is allowed to do

It answers questions about conditions and beach safety and is built to point you to the flags and the lifesavers. It is not designed to give a swim or don’t-swim verdict, and the answer to a question it cannot answer is to check with the patrol.

Shark Watch

Reports from strangers

Sightings come from other beachgoers and are unverified. A report is a reason to look, not a fact. If lifesavers or lifeguards close the beach, their call overrides anything in the app.

Over-reliance

Trusting the screen too much

The risk with any safety tool is that people stop looking at the water. Three design choices push against that: no safe state, a confidence figure on every outline so the model’s uncertainty is visible, and the flags line under each card.

If it happens

If you’re caught in a rip

This is the advice Surf Life Saving Australia gives. It works whether or not you have your phone with you.

  1. Stay calm and float

    A rip pulls you away from shore, not under the water. Conserve your energy.

  2. Don’t swim against it

    Strong swimmers tire fast fighting a current head-on.

  3. Swim parallel to the beach

    Move sideways until you are out of the channel, then angle back towards shore.

  4. Can’t get out? Raise an arm

    Keep floating, raise one arm and call for help.

Source: Surf Life Saving Australia, beachsafe.org.au.
