About

Why I built RipCo

RipCo is my Year 12 Design and Technology major work: a free iPhone app that reads public beach cameras and outlines likely rip currents on the live view. This page covers the need, the research, the criteria, the decisions and where the project is up to.

The need

People can’t see rips, and the cameras that can are already streaming

Rip currents are Australia’s number one coastal hazard. Surf Life Saving Australia’s National Coastal Safety Report 2025 counted 154 coastal drowning deaths in 2024–25, the highest number on record, with a rip involved in more than one in three beach drowning deaths.1 Its 2018 coastal safety survey found that three in four people could not spot a rip from the beach.2

Between the flags on a patrolled beach, lifesavers and lifeguards are reading the water for you. Outside patrol hours, or away from the flags, you are on your own judgement, and the 2025 report notes that the coastal drowning deaths it recorded happened at unpatrolled locations, outside patrol hours or away from the flags.1

Most people cannot read a rip from the sand, and many beaches already have a public camera pointed at the surf. RipCo reads those feeds.

Who it is for, and when

A beachgoer at home, deciding whether to go

The user is someone who swims at a Sydney beach and checks conditions before leaving, which 69% of the people I surveyed already do. They open the app at home, in the morning, with one question: what is the water doing at my beach? They may look again on the sand, in bright sun, with one hand free.

That context set three constraints for the app. The headline has to be readable in one glance, so each beach card leads with the risk score and the Live tag. The reminder to check the flags sits under every card, because the decision to swim is made at the beach. And the detection view is one tap from any screen, because the outline is the thing the survey respondents said they would use.

Process

How the project ran

Six stages, from a hazard most people cannot see to a prototype running on a real camera. Each one fed the next, and the criteria came out of the first two.

  1. Identify the need

    Rips are the number one hazard on Australian beaches, and most people cannot pick one from the sand. The published reports set the scale; my survey confirmed it locally.

  2. Research

    A survey of beachgoers, interviews with Surf Life Saving NSW patrol members, and the national coastal safety reports.

  3. Concept

    Use the public cameras already pointed at the surf, and put the reading on the phone people already carry to the beach.

  4. Prototype

    An iPhone app with live cameras, conditions, a risk score and alerts, and a computer-vision model developed on footage from a public camera at Manly Beach.

  5. Test

    The model run on the Manly camera and its outlines checked against the water, with the limits recorded as they appeared: night, glare, rain and distance.

  6. Evaluate and refine

    The prototype judged against the design criteria, with the next steps set: open the early-access list, submit to App Review and test the next beach.

Research

Survey, interviews, reading

Two pieces of primary research shaped the app, alongside the published reports. The full method and results are in my design folio. The sample was a convenience sample and the interviews were few, so the figures show direction, not precision.

Primary · Survey

Survey of beachgoers

I asked beachgoers how they check conditions and whether they could pick a rip. 78% could not confidently identify a rip current from the beach; 84% said they would use a mobile tool that shows rips on a live camera view; 69% already check conditions before they go.

What it changed: the outline on the live view (decision 02) and the one-screen home (criterion 5).

Primary · Interviews

Surf Life Saving NSW patrol members

Interviews with Surf Life Saving NSW patrol members shaped the safety commitment: no “safe” verdict anywhere in the app, and the reminder to check the flags on the home screen and on each beach card. The views are mine, and nothing on this site is endorsed by Surf Life Saving.

What it changed: risk instead of safety (decision 03) and the flags line under every card (decision 04).

Secondary

Reports and research

Surf Life Saving Australia’s National Coastal Safety Reports and its coastal safety survey, the rip current research of the UNSW Beach Safety Research Group, and BeachSafe’s public beach information. They are listed under sources.

What it changed: the three visual cues the model looks for, and the scale of the need on the home page.

Design decisions

Eight choices, and where each came from

Each one names where it came from. Most came out of the research; the rest came from testing on the Manly camera, where a missed rip on a calm-looking day would be the worst outcome.

01

Use cameras that already exist

New hardware would need money, permission and maintenance at every beach. Public cameras already look at the surf, so the cost of adding a beach is software and testing.

From: the need, and the budget of a student project.

02

Show an outline and a confidence figure

A tick or a cross would ask you to trust the model. An outline shows where it is looking and the percentage shows how sure it is, so you can check both against what you see.

From: the survey, where 78% could not pick a rip and 84% wanted it shown on the camera.

03

A risk score instead of a safety rating

Each beach card carries a score out of 100 built from the live conditions and labelled as a level of risk. The lowest band is still a level of risk, and the word safe does not appear in the app. How the score is built.

From: the patrol-member interviews.

04

Flags first, under every card

The line “check the flags” sits under each beach card, with the patrol status where it is known, because the app’s job is to send people towards the patrolled area.

From: the patrol-member interviews and Surf Life Saving guidance.

05

Free, with no tiers

A safety tool behind a paywall protects the people who pay. RipCo has no subscription and no premium version.

From: the survey, where 84% said they would use a free mobile tool.

06

One beach and one platform first

Manly Beach and iPhone, so I could train and test detection in one place before adding anything else.

From: testing on the Manly camera.

07

Live means the camera, not the model

The Live tag on a beach card says the camera is streaming. Detection is shown separately, on the Detect screen, only where I have tested it, so a stream is never mistaken for a checked beach.

From: testing, where most cameras stream and one has been checked.

08

Unknown defaults to the flags

Where the app cannot confirm a patrol it says “Patrol unknown” and gives the same instruction as everywhere else: check the flags. Missing information never reads as reassurance.

From: the patrol-member interviews.

Evaluation

What I would change, and what the prototype cannot yet claim

One beach
Detection has been tested on one camera. The next beach will need its own footage and its own test.
Camera uptime
The feed belongs to someone else. If it goes down, so does detection at that beach.
iPhone only
One platform kept the build testable in the time available. Android users are excluded for now.
Live video
Streaming costs data and battery on the sand. A lower-rate mode would help.
Shown to users
Not yet beyond the interviews. Showing the build to survey respondents is the next evaluation step.
Sustainability and ethics

Nothing manufactured, nothing mounted

The choices that keep the project small are the same ones that make it responsible.

Environmental

Reuse, not hardware

The app reads cameras that already exist and runs on a phone the user already owns. Nothing is built, powered or maintained on the beach.

Social

Free, and pointed at the flags

No paywall, no tiers, and a design whose job is to send people to the patrolled area. The data it shows comes from public sources.

Economic

Software, not sites

Adding a beach costs testing time, not equipment. The site itself runs on static files with no tracking and no third-party scripts.

The ethics questions a safety app raises, from using someone else’s camera to putting an assistant in the app, are on the safety page.

About this site

Static HTML, one stylesheet and a little JavaScript, hosted on Vercel in Sydney. No analytics, no trackers, no third-party scripts, and type served from this domain: Newsreader, by Production Type, and Geist, by Vercel, both under the SIL Open Font License. Warm paper, ink and one ocean-blue accent. Green, amber and red appear only where they carry a safety meaning, on this site and in the app: the flags, the detection outline and the risk label.
